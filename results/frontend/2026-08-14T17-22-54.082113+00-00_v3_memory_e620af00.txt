In the context of food safety regulations in Utah (R3/92-100), **cold holding** refers to the practice of maintaining Time/Temperature Control for Safety (TCS) foods at specific, safe temperatures—typically **41°F (5°C) or below**—to prevent the rapid growth of harmful bacteria.

Based on the Utah Food Service Sanitation Rule, here are the key components of cold holding:

*   **Temperature Control:** The primary goal is to ensure that food remains out of the "danger zone." For example, regulations for refrigerated vending machines specify that ambient air temperature should not exceed 5°C (41°F) for extended periods.
*   **Equipment Standards:** Cold holding equipment must be designed and maintained so that sensors can accurately measure the temperature in the warmest part of the unit. This ensures that even the least-chilled part of the storage area remains safe.
*   **Relationship to Cooling:** Cold holding is often the final step after a "cooling" process. For certain foods, such as those in reduced oxygen packaging, the food must be cooled to 41°F (5°C) or below before being held at that temperature for long-term storage.
*   **Prevention of Contamination:** Proper cold holding also involves managing how food is arranged in equipment (e.g., using shallow pans or leaving containers loosely covered during cooling) to facilitate heat transfer and prevent overhead contamination.

In short, "cold holding" is a critical preventive control used by restaurants to ensure that perishable foods stay at temperatures low enough to inhibit pathogen growth throughout their entire period of service.
